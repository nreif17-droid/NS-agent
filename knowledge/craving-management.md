# Craving / Urge Management

**New file, added 2026-08-06**, following up on a distinction that surfaced in real use (`logs/self/2026-08-06T04-33-20Z.md`): a day can be functionally strong — real demands handled well, no acute overwhelm — and still end in relapse, because the craving itself was running on a mechanism largely independent of situational stress. Everything in `protocols-generic.md`/`protocols-basic.md` targets stress/arousal regulation (ANS/HPA). Craving is a related but distinct mechanism (reward/cue-learning circuitry), and needed its own file rather than being force-fit into the existing protocols.

> Source-quality key: 🟢 strong 🟡 moderate 🔴 weak/fringe — see `foundations.md`. **Extra caution flag for this file specifically:** a lot of web content on "craving duration" and "how to beat cravings" comes from commercial addiction-treatment marketing sites, not primary research. Where that's the best source found this pass, it's marked down accordingly rather than presented with false confidence.

## 1. Why craving can show up without a situational trigger

Craving runs substantially on reward/cue-learning circuitry, not primarily the sympathetic/HPA machinery covered in `foundations.md`. Neuroimaging of cue-reactivity in nicotine addiction implicates a specific network — amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, ventral striatum — and reduced prefrontal reactivity to cues is associated with *higher* cue-induced craving (less top-down regulation of the cue response). 🟢 ([cue reactivity network — PMC3014110](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3014110/), [dissociable cortical control of cue reactivity — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21429478/))

Critically, "cues" aren't only external (a place, a person, seeing the substance) — they can be internal states: time of day, fatigue, low mood. This is the mechanistic basis for a craving that shows up as background "ache" without a specific identifiable trigger — it doesn't require an external stressor to fire. 🟢 (mechanism) — the specific claim that evening hours are a common cannabis-craving-associated time (via learned association with prior use patterns) is directionally plausible and matches commonly reported patterns, but the best source found this pass for that specific claim was a commercial recovery-content site, not a peer-reviewed study — flagged 🟡 accordingly, not elevated beyond what it's actually sourced from.

## 2. Time course of craving

**What's well-sourced:** an ecological momentary assessment (EMA) pilot study tracking hourly craving/withdrawal/affect during early nicotine abstinence found craving was measurably greater at 1–4 hours of abstinence, and withdrawal/negative affect greater after 3+ hours, compared to moderate smokers — real hour-scale dynamics from actual repeated real-world sampling, not a lab guess. 🟢 ([naturalistic craving EMA pilot study — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25844632/))

**Where this pass disagrees with popular claims:** it's commonly stated (mostly on rehab-marketing sites) that "a craving lasts 3–5 minutes" or "peaks and resolves within 20 minutes." That specific numeric claim did not trace back to a primary study in this pass — it's a popularized simplification, not a sourced figure. What the actual EMA/lab literature supports is the *shape*, not a precise duration: craving rises and falls rather than staying at a fixed plateau, and lab-based cue-exposure studies have found cue-triggered craving still elevated at the 20–30 minute mark in some cases — longer than the popular "few minutes" framing suggests. 🟡 **Practical implication: don't promise a specific countdown timer; the honest claim is "it will not stay at peak intensity indefinitely," not "it'll be over in exactly N minutes."**

**Incubation of craving — a real, counter-intuitive finding worth stating plainly:** for cocaine, nicotine, methamphetamine, and alcohol, cue-triggered craving/reward-seeking has been shown to *increase*, not just fade, over the first weeks to months of abstinence, before eventually declining — first described in animal models (Grimm et al., 2001) and since replicated in human translational studies using self-report and EEG (cue-induced late positive potential amplitude) measures. 🟢 ([incubation of drug craving in humans — PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11060480/)) **This matters for framing:** it means "it gets easier every single day" isn't reliably true for cue-triggered craving specifically — some people's cue-reactivity gets *stronger* before it fades. Worth knowing so an upswing in craving intensity at weeks (not days) into a taper isn't mistaken for failure or reversal.

## 3. Urge surfing — the best-evidenced technique for this specific problem

Developed by Alan Marlatt (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985) as part of Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention: instead of suppressing or fighting a craving, observe it as a physical sensation that rises, peaks, and falls like a wave, without acting on it. 🟢 (well-established clinical technique)

**Direct RCT evidence, and an important nuance:** Bowen & Marlatt's cue-exposure RCT in undergraduate smokers (n=123) found the mindfulness/urge-surfing group did **not** show significantly lower subjective urge ratings than controls — but they smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over the following 7 days. 🟢 **This is worth stating honestly rather than oversold:** the technique's real evidence is for *changing what you do in response to the urge*, not for making the urge itself feel smaller. That's a meaningfully different (and more honest) claim than "this technique makes cravings go away." ([Surfing the Urge RCT — ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/40755906_Surfing_the_Urge_Brief_Mindfulness-Based_Intervention_for_College_Student_Smokers))

Broader Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) programs (typically 8 weeks) show reduced craving and improved stress management with effects sustained at 2–4 month follow-up in some studies. 🟡 (moderate — smaller trials, not a large multi-site meta-analytic base) ([MBRP mechanisms review — PMC5441879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5441879/))

## 4. Sleep — the mechanism directly relevant to the logged pattern

This is the best-sourced, most directly applicable finding in this file given what's already in the log:

- A systematic review of human studies found cannabis withdrawal is reliably associated with disrupted sleep — reduced total sleep time, prolonged sleep-onset latency, and REM rebound. 🟢 ([cannabis withdrawal and sleep, systematic review — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25893849/))
- In survey data, 65% of respondents reported that sleep difficulty during a past abstinence attempt contributed to relapse on at least one prior quit attempt. 🟢 ([sleep disturbances and cannabis use/cessation — Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40429-014-0016-9))
- **The vicious-cycle mechanism:** cannabis is often used, in part, for its sleep-promoting effect; tolerance develops to that effect with regular use; on cessation, the withdrawal-driven sleep disruption arrives at exactly the moment the (subjectively effective, if tolerance-diminished) sleep aid is removed — compounding rather than independent problems. 🟢 (same source)

**This directly strengthens, not just plausibly relates to, the working hypothesis already logged on 2026-08-06:** poor sleep isn't just generically bad for regulation capacity (`dysregulation-causes.md` §1.2) — for cannabis specifically, it's a documented, named relapse-risk pathway in its own right. Two independent mechanisms point the same direction, which is a stronger basis for prioritizing sleep this week than either one alone would be.

## 5. A practical urge-surfing sequence, built from the cited mechanism above

This sequence itself is original synthesis (not independently studied as a bundle) — the same disclosure as `regularization-procedure.md`/`optimization-procedure.md`. Components are cited above.

1. **Name it** — "craving present," rather than immediately acting or immediately white-knuckling against it.
2. **Locate it physically** — where in the body is this showing up? Treating it as a physical sensation with a shape (rise/peak/fall), not a permanent state, is the actual mechanism behind urge surfing (§3).
3. **Breathe through it, don't fight it** — extended-exhale breathing (`protocols-generic.md` §1) alongside observing, not as a way to force the craving to stop.
4. **Let time pass without a promised countdown** — per §2, resist framing this as "just 5 more minutes," since that specific number isn't well-sourced; the honest frame is "this will not stay at peak intensity indefinitely."
5. **No moral scoring** — craving is a documented neurological event (§1), not a character failure. This matters practically: self-judgment doesn't have evidence behind it as a craving-reduction tool, and add-on shame is its own separate thing to regulate.

## Safety / framing note

This doesn't change the standing safety-flag list in `README.md`. Per `dysregulation-causes.md` §1.9, nicotine and cannabis withdrawal (craving included) are real but not medically dangerous, unlike alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal. Craving management protocols here don't trigger a doctor/therapist redirect on their own — only genuine escalation beyond the documented pattern does.

## Flagged claims summary

- 🔴/🟡 **"A craving lasts exactly N minutes"** — popular claim, best sources found this pass are commercial rehab-content sites, not primary research. The defensible claim is the *shape* (rises and falls), not a specific duration.
- 🟢 **Incubation of craving** — cue-triggered craving can *increase*, not just fade, over the first weeks/months of abstinence for several substances. Important so an upswing isn't mistaken for reversal.
- 🟡 **"Evening hours as a cannabis-specific craving trigger time"** — plausible via cue-learning mechanisms and matches commonly reported experience, but sourced this pass from a commercial recovery-content site, not a peer-reviewed study specifically on time-of-day craving patterns.
- 🟡 **Urge surfing reduces the urge itself** — the actual RCT evidence shows reduced *use*, not reduced *subjective urge intensity*. Worth not conflating the two when describing the technique.
